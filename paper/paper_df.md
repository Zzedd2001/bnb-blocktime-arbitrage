# Faster Blocks, Diminishing Returns: Arbitrage Rents and LP Costs across Three Block-Interval Reductions on BNB Chain

*Submission to Digital Finance — manuscript v5.0 (September 2026)*

## Abstract

Automated market makers quote stale prices between blocks, and the fee-band model of arbitrage predicts that the mispricing at which arbitrageurs trade — the source of liquidity providers' (LPs') adverse-selection cost — scales with the square root of the block interval. We test this on three pre-announced reductions of BNB Chain's block interval, from 3 s to 1.5, 0.75 and 0.45 s, on seven PancakeSwap v3 pools, with 49 million swaps aligned to Binance trades at millisecond resolution. The mispricing falls at every fork, but by a diminishing fraction of the prediction: 84% at the first halving, 28–59% and 44–51% at the two sub-second reductions. Reconstructing when each of 6.5 million arbitrage opportunities opened shows why: arbitrageurs respond with a fixed latency of 0.1–0.35 s, and only the mispricing that accumulates while they wait for a block shrinks by the law; the jump of the reference price that opens an opportunity and the overshoot of same-block back-runs do not respond to the clock, and their share rises from 32% at 3-second blocks to 44% at 0.75 s. The diminishing returns are a composition effect; a further halving would remove roughly a tenth of the remaining mispricing. Arbitrageurs' profits fall by 8–22% per fork and mispricing episodes shorten by 20–35%, but LPs' gross adverse-selection loss does not fall and, in the 5 bp pools, equals their fee income: the block interval is a lever over price efficiency and arbitrage rents, not over the cost of liquidity provision.

**Keywords:** automated market makers; loss-versus-rebalancing; block time; arbitrage latency; decentralized exchanges; natural experiment

**JEL classification:** D47; G12; G14; G23; O33

## 1. Introduction

Automated market makers (AMMs) quote prices mechanically and can only be updated by trades. Between two blocks, the external price moves but the pool price does not, so the first trader to act on the new block can buy from (or sell to) the pool at a stale price. Milionis, Moallemi, Roughgarden and Zhang (2022) named the liquidity providers' cost of this stale quoting *loss-versus-rebalancing* (LVR) and showed that, without fees, it is a function of price volatility alone. Once trading fees are introduced, arbitrageurs only act when the mispricing exceeds the fee band, and the block interval becomes a market-design parameter: the shorter the interval, the less the price can drift out of the band before the next opportunity to correct it (Milionis, Moallemi and Roughgarden, 2025). The policy implication has been drawn many times. Fritsch and Canidio (2024) simulate that reducing Ethereum's 12-second blocks to 100 milliseconds would cut LPs' arbitrage losses by 20–70%; layer-2 sequencers advertise sub-second blocks as an LP-friendly feature; and proposals for batch auctions, dynamic fees and auction-managed AMMs are all motivated by the rent that discrete blocks hand to arbitrageurs.

Whether faster blocks actually help LPs, and whether they keep helping as blocks get very fast, are empirical questions that have been hard to answer. Cross-chain comparisons confound block time with everything else that differs between chains — user base, gas costs, ordering rules, the set of arbitrageurs — and simulations rely on the assumed behaviour of arbitrageurs. This paper uses a setting in which block time changed and nothing else about the market did. BNB Chain shortened its block interval three times within nine months, from 3 s to 1.5 s (Lorentz, 29 April 2025), to 0.75 s (Maxwell, 30 June 2025) and to 0.45 s (Fermi, 14 January 2026), by pre-announced hard forks whose timing was set by an engineering roadmap and not by market conditions. The protocol (PancakeSwap v3), the pools, the fee tiers, the liquidity providers and the arbitrageurs were the same before and after each fork. We study all three events on seven PancakeSwap v3 pools covering four fee tiers and 49 million swaps, with the last Binance trade before each swap's millisecond block timestamp as the external price.

The fee-band model makes sharp, quantitative predictions that we can take to these events. First, the *overshoot* — how far beyond the fee band the price has drifted when an arbitrageur finally trades — should scale with σ√Δt, so its log should fall by ½·ln(Δt₁/Δt₀): −0.347 for a halving (Lorentz, Maxwell) and −0.255 for the 40% reduction of Fermi. Second, arbitrageurs' profit rate should scale with σ³√Δt/γ and fall by the same log amount at a given volatility. Third, the LPs' gross adverse-selection loss — what the pool loses relative to the external price on the trades it executes — equals the arbitrageurs' profit plus the fees they pay, and only the first part depends on Δt; its predicted change is the arbitrageurs' share of the gross loss times the change in that share's own law, a few percent at sub-second blocks and more at longer ones. Fourth, because the predictions are multiplicative, the log effects should be the same across pools with different fee tiers and volatilities and across high- and low-volatility hours. Fifth — the test that three forks make possible — the elasticity of the overshoot to Δt should be the same 0.5 at every point of the roadmap from 3 s to 0.45 s, unless arbitrageurs face a delay that does not shrink with the block interval, in which case the elasticity should fall as blocks get faster.

Our identification is a before–after comparison in an hourly pool panel around each fork, controlling for the hour's realised volatility on Binance, active liquidity, trading volume, time-of-day fixed effects and pool fixed effects, with standard errors clustered by day. We use ±14-day and ±30-day windows, complement the panel with same-weekday-hour matched pairs and a regression-discontinuity-style jump with separate pre- and post-trends, and run a fake-fork placebo inside each pre-period to check which specifications manufacture an effect out of the ambient trends. The confounders are real and different across the three events: a wash-trading cycle tied to Binance's Alpha rewards programme started two weeks after Lorentz and receded during the Maxwell window, and the crypto sell-off of early February 2026 doubled volatility in the second half of the Fermi window. The specification that passes the placebo tests is different for each fork, and we report the full grid of windows and controls for all three.

The results are as follows. The strict overshoot (the mispricing at arbitrage trades that land the price back inside the band) fell after every fork, but not by the same fraction of the prediction. After Lorentz the placebo-clean estimate is −0.291 (s.e. 0.041), 84% of the predicted −0.347 — 60% on the trades of arbitrage contracts alone, once the order flow that reaches the pools through public routers is excluded (Section 6.7); after Maxwell it is −0.099 to −0.206, 28–59% of the same prediction; after Fermi −0.112 to −0.131, 44–51% of the predicted −0.255. As elasticities with respect to Δt these are 0.42 (0.05–0.06), 0.14–0.30 and 0.22–0.26 against the √Δt law's 0.5; the precision-weighted pooled elasticity is 0.15–0.38 depending on the specification, five to ten standard errors below 0.5, and the hypothesis that the three forks share one elasticity is rejected at the 10% level in ten of eleven specifications (at 5% in nine), and in five of the six specifications without a trend once router flow is excluded. The pattern — a first halving that delivers most of the √Δt law and two further reductions that deliver roughly a third to a half of it — is what a fixed arbitrageur latency would produce, and a one-parameter extension in which the effective interval is Δt + ℓ fits the three estimates with ℓ between 0.6 and 1.1 seconds, 1.0 s on the trades of arbitrage contracts alone (the pure √Δt law, ℓ = 0, is rejected with χ² of 18 or more on three degrees of freedom). Taking the reference price 250 milliseconds before the block timestamp, closer to the moment at which the arbitrageur observed it, raises the two sub-second estimates by 0.01–0.04 and leaves every one of these conclusions in place.

We then open up the overshoot. For each of 6.5 million trades by arbitrage contracts we reconstruct, from Binance's millisecond trade record and the pool's own trade history, the moment at which the opportunity opened — the last time the reference price crossed out of the pool's no-arbitrage band, or the block in which another trade pushed the pool price out of it — the response time until the arbitrage landed, and the number of blocks that elapsed in between. Three things follow. First, the arbitrageurs' fixed latency is directly measurable and small: at the two sub-second forks the median response time falls by close to half the change in the block interval, as a fixed latency implies, the intercepts of its quantiles are 0.1–0.35 s at sub-second block times and, at Fermi, move by less than 50 ms at the fork — down from 0.3–1.4 s in April 2025, when the same arbitrageurs were much slower — and the share of arbitrages that miss the first block after the opportunity opens rises from 14–47% at 3-second blocks to 37–51% at 0.45-second blocks. Second, the response times account for the whole of the Lorentz effect but for only part of the sub-second shortfall: in the 5 bp pools the fork effect they imply is −0.16 to −0.24 at Maxwell and Fermi against estimates of −0.04 to −0.18. Third, and decisive, the overshoot decomposes into a part that accumulates while the arbitrageur waits — 28% of the strict overshoot in the four liquid pools, 32% in the three core pools — and parts that do not depend on the block interval at all: the jump of the reference price at the trade that opens the opportunity (19%), and the overshoots of arbitrages triggered on chain, by a same-block trade (18%) or by an earlier partial correction (34%). The first part falls with the block interval at every fork — −0.46, −0.19 and −0.32 log points against the law's −0.35, −0.35 and −0.26, i.e. by the law or more at Lorentz and Fermi and by half of it at Maxwell; the jump component moves by −0.07 to +0.04 and the back-run component by −0.14 to +0.06 at the two sub-second forks. Weighting each component's effect by its pre-fork share reproduces the total effect at each fork. The diminishing returns are a composition effect: the components that do not respond make up 32% of the overshoot in the fortnight before Lorentz, at 3-second blocks, and 44% in the fortnight before Fermi, at 0.75-second blocks, and a further halving to 0.225 s would remove about a tenth of the remaining mispricing. Arbitrageurs' hourly profits fell by 22% after Lorentz, 20% after Maxwell in the specifications that allow for the receding activity cycle, and 8–13% after Fermi. Mispricing episodes — runs of seconds during which the pool price sits outside the fee band — became 20–35% shorter in most pools. The LPs' gross adverse-selection loss changed by −6% after Lorentz and by between −3% and +7% after Maxwell and Fermi, and their net return per unit of volume did not improve measurably in the 5 bp pools outside the Lorentz activity wave; across the six block-interval regimes from 3 s to 0.45 s, the LPs' loss in the ETH and BTCB 5 bp pools is 79–120% of their fee income. There is little heterogeneity of the log effect by volatility or time of day at the two later forks, and the three core pools give coefficients of the same sign at each fork although their exposures σ√Δt/γ differ by a factor of 1.5–3.

A methodological result runs through the paper and changes the substantive ones. The external price is usually taken from 1-second (or 1-minute) candles; we estimate everything twice, with the close of the last completed 1-second candle before the block timestamp and with the last individual Binance trade before it. The candle reference goes stale by the fractional second of the block timestamp, up to one second. When an arbitrage trade responds to a move of the external price that occurred after the candle closed, the stale reference understates the deviation the arbitrageur acted on, or fails to classify the trade as an arbitrage at all; and the share of arbitrage trades that respond to moves within the last second rises when blocks get faster, because arbitrageurs act sooner after the move. The candle reference therefore manufactures part of the effect of a fork. Measured hour by hour in the same panel, the wedge between the two references jumps on the fork day by 0.13 (0.02) log points at Maxwell and 0.08 (0.03) at Fermi with no pre-trend, by 0.22–0.32 for profits and 0.05–0.12 for the count of arbitrages; with the candle reference the Maxwell and Fermi overshoot effects were 58–101% and 76–87% of the prediction and the profit falls 14–45% (against 8–22% with the trade reference), and the count of arbitrages appeared to fall after Fermi. Lorentz supplies a clean test of the mechanism: before it, blocks were stamped in whole seconds, so the candle and the trade reference coincide exactly and the wedge is identically zero; after it, half the blocks carry a half-second stamp, the reference gap appears, and the wedge rises to 0.03. The magnitudes of the bias at the three forks are ordered as the mechanism predicts.

The paper contributes to three literatures. To the theory of AMM design (Milionis et al., 2022, 2025; Capponi and Jia, 2025; Hasbrouck, Rivera and Saleh, 2026a; Adams et al., 2026), it provides the first within-chain causal estimates of how the block interval enters arbitrageurs' rents and LPs' costs, at three doses, and finds the √Δt law where the theory places it — in the part of the mispricing that accumulates while arbitrageurs wait for a block — while showing, with the first direct measurement of arbitrageurs' response times on a public chain, that the aggregate response flattens below about one second because the discreteness of the reference price and the on-chain triggers of arbitrage, not only the arbitrageurs' latency, put a floor under the mispricing; and it finds that LPs' cost is affected only to second order, as the fee band implies. To the empirical literature on DEX market quality and price discovery (Lehar and Parlour, 2025; Barbon and Ranaldo, 2026; Capponi, Jia and Yu, 2026; Alexander et al., 2025; Fritsch and Canidio, 2024), it adds a natural experiment in which the friction that separates DEX from CEX prices was changed exogenously three times, shows that faster blocks improve price efficiency at the pool without redistributing the cost of adverse selection, and documents that the arbitrage losses of LPs in 5 bp pools are of the size of their fee income. To the measurement literature on MEV and arbitrage (Zhou et al., 2021; Qin et al., 2022; Öz et al., 2025), it shows that at sub-second block times the resolution and alignment of the reference price are first-order: a 1-second candle understates arbitrage activity and LP adverse selection by up to a third and biases the estimated effect of any change in block time; and it documents, at the level of contracts and of the operators behind them, that a handful of operators execute most CEX–DEX arbitrage, that their latency fell steadily between April 2025 and January 2026 — from about a second to 0.15 s for the largest arbitrageur of the ETH pool, from 0.44 s to 0.06 s in the BTCB pool for the largest overall — and that at 0.45-second blocks the fastest of them sign from pools of dozens of wallets to keep several transactions in flight.

## 2. Institutional Setting

### 2.1 BNB Chain's block-interval roadmap

BNB Smart Chain runs a proof-of-staked-authority consensus (Parlia) with a fixed set of validators who produce blocks in turn at a fixed nominal interval. In 2025–26 the chain implemented a three-stage roadmap toward sub-second blocks (Table 1; BNB Chain, 2025a, 2025b, 2026). The Lorentz hard fork (BEP-520, 29 April 2025 05:05 UTC, block 48,773,576) halved the interval from 3 s to 1.5 s and introduced millisecond timestamps; the Maxwell hard fork (BEP-524, 30 June 2025 02:30 UTC, block 52,337,091) halved it again to 0.75 s; the Fermi hard fork (BEP-619, 14 January 2026 02:30 UTC, block 75,140,593) reduced it to 0.45 s. Each fork was announced weeks in advance with a fixed activation time; the timing followed client releases and validator upgrades, not market conditions.

Two features of the forks matter for interpretation. First, each fork bundled other changes. Lorentz cut the per-block gas limit from 140 million to 70 million, keeping per-second throughput unchanged (validators raised the limit back to 100 million a month later); Maxwell lengthened the number of consecutive blocks a validator produces (from 8 to 16, keeping each validator's turn at 12 seconds), shortened finality, and cut the gas limit from 125 million to 75 million (the limit had been raised from 100 to 125 million on 4 June 2025); Fermi cut the gas limit from 100 million to 55 million and revised the fast-finality voting rules (BEP-590) alongside client-side optimisations (BEP-592/593/610). Per-second gas throughput was therefore unchanged at Lorentz, rose by 20% at Maxwell and fell by 8% at Fermi. Figure 1 shows that daily gas utilisation stayed between 9% and 61% around the forks, so none of the changes created congestion, and none of the bundled changes touches how a swap is priced. Second, the forks were clean in the sense that matters for our design — the same validators, the same PancakeSwap contracts, the same LPs and the same arbitrageurs operated before and after — but they were not the only thing happening. Section 5 and Figure A1 document the confounders, which differ across the three events and are largest around Lorentz.

## 3. Model and Predictions

### 3.1 Arbitrage against an AMM with fees and discrete blocks

Consider a pool with fee γ (5 bp for the core pools) whose price can only change when a block is produced, every Δt seconds. Let the log external price follow a Brownian motion with volatility σ (per √second). Define the mispricing as the log distance between the pool price and the external price. An arbitrageur who trades against the pool pays the fee, so a trade is profitable only when the mispricing exceeds γ; when it does, the arbitrageur trades until the mispricing is back at the edge of the band. Between blocks the mispricing drifts freely; at a block it is reset to the band edge if it lies outside.

In the fast-block regime, σ√Δt ≪ γ, this process has a simple structure (Milionis, Moallemi and Roughgarden, 2025). The mispricing behaves like a Brownian motion reflected at ±γ, so it is approximately uniformly distributed over the band. A block contains a profitable trade only if the mispricing was within roughly σ√Δt of an edge at the previous block and moved outward since, so the probability that a given block is profitable is of order σ√Δt/γ. Conditional on a profitable block, the amount by which the mispricing exceeds the band — the *overshoot* that the arbitrageur captures — is the overshoot of a Gaussian increment beyond a barrier, of order σ√Δt. Two of the model's objects therefore scale with the square root of the block interval:

- **Overshoot at arbitrage.** E[overshoot | arbitrage] ∝ σ√Δt.
- **Arbitrageur profit rate.** Profit per unit time = (profit per profitable block) × (profitable blocks per unit time) ∝ [L·(σ√Δt)²] × [σ√Δt/γ] / Δt ∝ L·σ³√Δt/γ, where L is the pool's active liquidity. Equivalently, the profit rate equals the no-fee LVR rate, ½σ²·L, discounted by the fraction of blocks that are profitable.

The LPs' cost is different. Measured against the external price, the pool loses money on an arbitrage trade because it sells at a price that is stale by γ + overshoot; the pool loses money on *any* trade executed while the pool price differs from the external price. Summed over all trades, this gross adverse-selection loss — the empirical counterpart of LVR when the pool's execution is marked to the external price — equals the arbitrageurs' profit Π plus the fees F they pay. In the fast-block limit, Π vanishes as √Δt, but F does not: the volume required to keep the mispricing inside the band is governed by the local time of the reflected process at the band edges, which is of order σ²/γ per unit time and independent of Δt. Writing s = Π/(Π + F) for the arbitrageurs' share of the gross loss, a change of the block interval from Δt₀ to Δt₁ changes the gross loss by

Δlog(Π + F) ≈ −s·(1 − √(Δt₁/Δt₀)),

i.e. by 0.29·s for a halving and 0.23·s for the Fermi reduction. The share s is itself increasing in Δt (Π ∝ √Δt while F is not), so the LPs' gain from a faster block is largest at long intervals and shrinks along the roadmap. Faster blocks transfer rent from arbitrageurs to the LPs' fee income, but the transfer is bounded by the arbitrageurs' profit, which in our data is 0.8–2.0 bp of pool volume at sub-second intervals against a gross loss of 3.5–5.4 bp.

### 3.2 A latency floor and a discreteness floor

The model's clock is the block interval: the arbitrageur observes the external price at the instant a block is produced and trades in it. Real arbitrageurs observe the CEX price with a delay, build and broadcast a transaction, and wait for it to be included; and validators assemble a block some time before its timestamp. If the sum of these delays is a constant ℓ that does not shrink with the block interval, the price the arbitrageur acts on is older than the block by ℓ on average, and the mispricing at the trade is the drift accumulated over Δt + ℓ rather than over Δt. The overshoot then scales with σ√(Δt + ℓ), a fork changes its log by ½·ln((Δt₁ + ℓ)/(Δt₀ + ℓ)), and the elasticity to Δt measured at a fork is ½·Δt/(Δt + ℓ) evaluated between the two intervals — 0.5 when Δt ≫ ℓ and approaching zero as Δt falls below ℓ. The extension has one parameter, is nested in the model at ℓ = 0, and predicts a specific ordering across the three forks: the first halving from 3 s should deliver most of the √Δt law, and the reductions below 1.5 s progressively less.

The latency extension treats the overshoot as a single quantity that grows with the time the arbitrageur takes. It is not. Consider an arbitrage triggered by the reference price: the price crosses the edge of the band at t₀ and the arbitrage lands in a block at t₀ + τ. The overshoot at the trade is the sum of two pieces, |dev| − γ = J + M: the excess J by which the crossing trade itself carried the reference price beyond the band edge, and the movement M of the reference price between the crossing and the block. M accumulates over the response time τ, so it is the part to which the √Δt logic applies: with τ = ℓ + wait and the wait spread over a block interval, M behaves as the previous paragraph describes. J does not depend on the block interval at all: it is set by the size distribution of trades on the reference exchange — the discreteness of the reference price — and by nothing on the chain. Two further kinds of arbitrage are triggered not by the reference price but by the pool's own trades: a swap that pushes the pool price out of the band and is corrected in the same block (a back-run), and an arbitrage that completes a correction begun in an earlier block (a continuation). The overshoot of a back-run is set by the size of the trade it corrects and does not respond to the clock; that of a continuation is the residual of the earlier correction plus the drift over the blocks since, and responds partly.

The aggregate elasticity of the overshoot to Δt is therefore the elasticity of M weighted by the share w_M of M in the total, plus the smaller response of the continuations. Since M shrinks with Δt while J and the back-runs do not, w_M falls along the roadmap, and the aggregate elasticity falls with it even if arbitrageurs' latency were zero. A latency floor and a discreteness floor produce the same pattern in the aggregate — a first halving that delivers most of the law, later reductions that deliver less — and can only be told apart by opening up the overshoot: the first is identified from the distribution of response times, the second from the components' separate responses to a fork. Section 4.5 constructs both and Section 6.9 reports them.

### 3.3 Predictions

Let Δt₀ and Δt₁ be the block intervals before and after a fork and define θ = ½·ln(Δt₁/Δt₀): θ = −0.347 for Lorentz and Maxwell and θ = −0.255 for Fermi.

- **P1 (overshoot).** Holding σ fixed, the log overshoot at arbitrage changes by θ.
- **P2 (arbitrageur profit).** Holding σ, γ and L fixed, the log arbitrageur profit rate changes by θ.
- **P3 (LP cost).** The LPs' gross adverse-selection loss changes by −s·(1 − √(Δt₁/Δt₀)), where s is the arbitrageurs' pre-fork share of it: a second-order effect that is largest for Lorentz and smallest for Fermi. The LPs' net return per unit of volume rises by at most the arbitrageurs' lost profit per unit of volume.
- **P4 (multiplicativity).** The predicted changes are in logs and do not depend on σ, γ or L, so they should be the same across pools, volatility regimes and times of day; the elasticity of overshoot to σ should be 1.
- **P5 (dose–response across forks).** With ℓ = 0 the elasticity of the overshoot with respect to Δt, β/ln(Δt₁/Δt₀), is 0.5 at each fork. With ℓ > 0 it is ½·ln((Δt₁+ℓ)/(Δt₀+ℓ))/ln(Δt₁/Δt₀), which falls from fork to fork along the roadmap; the three estimates identify ℓ.
- **P6 (response times).** If arbitrageurs face a fixed latency ℓ and land in the first block sealed after their transaction is ready, the response time τ from the opening of an opportunity to the arbitrage is ℓ plus a wait uniform on [0, Δt]: at a fork its median falls by half the change in Δt, its quantiles net of q·Δt share one intercept ℓ that does not change at the fork, and the share of arbitrages that land in the first block after the opening, E[max(0, 1 − ℓ/Δt)], falls as Δt shrinks toward ℓ.
- **P7 (components).** At a fork only the accumulated-movement component M of the overshoot changes, by θ (or by ½·ln((Δt₁+ℓ)/(Δt₀+ℓ)) with a latency); the crossing-jump component J and the overshoot of same-block back-runs do not change; the total changes by the average of the components' changes weighted by their pre-fork shares.

The model also implies that the number of arbitrage trades per unit time is set by how often the price leaves the band, which depends on σ and γ but not on Δt to first order, while each trade is smaller: a decomposition of the arbitrage-attributed loss into frequency and size should show a stable frequency and a falling size.

Three features of the setting make these predictions unusually testable. The forks change Δt by known amounts, so P1 and P2 are point predictions rather than sign predictions; the arbitrageurs' share s is observable, so P3 is a point prediction too; and because the same pools were treated three times with known doses, P5 tests the functional form rather than the existence of the effect.

## 4. Data and Measurement

### 4.1 On-chain data

We collect every `Swap` event of the seven pools in a window of 720 hours before and after each fork: blocks 47,909,777–50,500,853 (30 March – 29 May 2025, 13,336,583 swaps) for Lorentz, blocks 50,609,843–55,792,812 (31 May – 30 July 2025, 20,608,008 swaps) for Maxwell and blocks 71,685,230–80,898,050 (15 December 2025 – 13 February 2026, 15,420,282 swaps) for Fermi — 49,364,873 swaps in all. Events were retrieved with `eth_getLogs` from a public RPC provider for Maxwell and from the Envio HyperSync indexed log service for Lorentz and Fermi; on a 20,000-block reference slice (6,510 swaps) the two sources return identical events field by field. Each event gives the post-trade √price, the signed token amounts, the active liquidity and the transaction hash; the pre-trade price is the post-trade price of the preceding swap in the same pool.

Block timestamps matter at the sub-second scale and are not returned by `eth_getLogs`. BNB Chain's consensus sets each block's timestamp to its parent's plus the nominal interval, deviating only when the chain lags behind wall-clock time, so the full timestamp vector can be reconstructed from sparse anchors: we fetch two consecutive headers every ten minutes (45,370 headers for Lorentz, 55,982 for Maxwell, 87,291 for Fermi), verify for every anchor-to-anchor span that the observed elapsed time equals the nominal one, and bisect the spans that do not until the irregular blocks are located. Millisecond timestamps exist since Lorentz; before Lorentz, blocks were stamped in whole seconds, which is exact for a 3-second schedule. Reconstruction is exact for 99.94% of consecutive header pairs on a sample of 278,000 fetched headers, and the reconstructed timestamps agree with the provider-supplied timestamps of every swap block for which both are available. Hourly mean intervals are 3,000.7 and 1,500.6 ms around Lorentz, 1,500.7 and 750.1 ms around Maxwell, and 750.1 and 450.2 ms around Fermi (Figure 1).

### 4.2 Two reference prices

The external price is Binance's spot market, the dominant venue for all five assets. We construct two reference prices for every swap from Binance's public data archive. The **trade reference**, used for all results in the body of the paper, is the price of the last aggregate trade (BNBUSDT, ETHUSDT, BTCUSDT; CAKEUSDT/BNBUSDT as a cross-rate for the CAKE/WBNB pool) executed at or before the swap's block timestamp, at millisecond resolution; the daily aggregate-trade files are collapsed to the last price of each millisecond before alignment. The **candle reference** is the close of the last *completed* 1-second candle before the block timestamp, which is the convention of the empirical literature and was used in an earlier version of this paper. Both avoid look-ahead. Hourly realised volatility σ is computed from 1-minute log returns and expressed per √second; it is the same, to three decimals, under either reference.

The two references differ by the staleness of the candle. The last completed candle closed at the whole second preceding the block timestamp, so the candle reference lags the block by the fractional part of the timestamp — between zero and one second — plus the gap to the last trade within that candle. Before Lorentz, timestamps are whole seconds and the two references coincide exactly. After Lorentz, timestamps advance in steps of 1.5 s, so half the blocks carry a half-second stamp; after Maxwell, in steps of 0.75 s (fractional parts of 0, .25, .5 and .75); after Fermi, in steps of 0.45 s (twenty different fractional parts). The mean staleness of the candle reference is therefore zero before Lorentz and about half a second at every regime after it, and the price moves inside that half-second are of order σ√0.5 ≈ 0.3–1 bp for the core pools. Table 7 (Section 6.4) reports the mean absolute gap between the two references at each swap: zero before Lorentz, 0.06–0.56 bp in the core pools in the 1.5-second and 0.75-second regimes of the Maxwell window, 0.34–0.94 bp before Fermi and 0.89–1.81 bp after it, when volatility had doubled. The gap is of the same order as the overshoot at strict arbitrage, which is 0.3–1.0 bp at sub-second intervals.

Staleness of this size would be harmless if it were unrelated to the block interval. It is not. An arbitrage trade responds to a move of the external price that occurred at most one block interval earlier; when that move occurred after the candle closed, the candle reference does not contain it, the deviation the arbitrageur acted on is understated, and the trade may not be classified as an arbitrage at all. The share of arbitrage trades whose triggering move postdates the candle close is larger the faster the blocks, because arbitrageurs act sooner after the move (if the response delay were uniform over one block interval, that share would be roughly one sixth at 1.5-second blocks, one half at 0.75 s and three quarters at 0.45 s). A fork therefore changes the candle reference's error in a direction that mimics the effect being estimated. Section 6.2 measures this wedge directly and shows what it did to the estimates.

The trade reference has its own noise floor — Binance's minimum price increment (0.1–0.2 bp for BNB, 0.03–0.05 bp for ETH, negligible for BTC), bid–ask bounce of the last trade, and the alignment of two clocks — but that floor is symmetric around the fork and does not depend on the fractional second of the timestamp. A separate question is whether the reference should be aligned to the block timestamp at all, since the arbitrageur observed the CEX price some time before the block was produced; Section 6.8 re-estimates everything with the reference taken 250 ms before the block timestamp. We return to the noise floor in Section 7.2.

### 4.3 Arbitrage identification and LP economics

For each swap we compute the pre-trade deviation dev_pre = p_pre/P − 1, where p_pre is the pool price before the trade and P the reference price. A swap is classified as an **arbitrage** if |dev_pre| > γ and the trade moves the pool price toward P; it is a **strict arbitrage** if in addition the post-trade deviation lies inside the band. The **overshoot** of an arbitrage is |dev_pre| − γ, the distance beyond the band at which the arbitrageur acted; averaged over strict arbitrages in an hour it is our empirical counterpart of E[overshoot | arbitrage].

LP economics are measured from the pool's side in quote-currency units. For a swap that changes the pool's reserves by (ΔQ, ΔB) in quote and base tokens, `lp_gain` = ΔQ + ΔB·P is the pool's mark-to-market gain at the reference price, `fee_income` = γ × input value, and the **adverse-selection loss** is `arb_loss` = fee_income − lp_gain: the amount by which the pool's execution fell short of the external price, gross of fees. Summed over all swaps in an hour it is the empirical counterpart of the gross loss Π + F of Section 3; summed over identified arbitrages it is the LPs' loss to arbitrageurs, and `arb_profit` = Σ_arbitrages (arb_loss − fee_income) is the arbitrageurs' profit net of the pool fee (gross of gas and CEX costs). The ratio of the two hourly sums is the empirical share s. We also compute LP net return per unit of volume, lp_gain/volume, and the loss per identified arbitrage.

Price efficiency is measured on a 1-second grid: the share of seconds in which |dev| > γ, the mean length of runs of consecutive seconds outside the band (the *episode length*), and the AR(1) half-life of the deviation; these statistics use the 1-second candle series by construction and are identical under either reference.

### 4.5 Opening times, response times and the components of the overshoot

For every identified arbitrage we reconstruct when the opportunity it closed had opened. Because the pool price is constant between the pool's own trades, the no-arbitrage band around the pre-trade pool price — the reference prices P with |p_pre/P − 1| ≤ γ — is known at every instant since the previous swap, and Binance's millisecond trade record tells us when the reference price last crossed out of it. We classify each arbitrage by its trigger. A *CEX-triggered* arbitrage is one for which the reference price crossed out of the band at some time t_open after the previous swap and stayed outside until the block; its response time is τ = t_block − t_open, and the crossing trade is located by a backward search over the tick series within 120 seconds of the block. An *on-chain-triggered* arbitrage is one whose previous swap, in an earlier block, pushed the pool price out of the band: the opportunity opened when that block was published, so τ = t_block − t_prev is a difference of two block timestamps and is immune to any clock offset between Binance and BNB Chain. A *continuation* is an arbitrage whose previous swap, in an earlier block, was itself an arbitrage that left the price outside the band (a partial correction) or pushed an open opportunity further out. A *same-block* arbitrage follows another swap of the pool in the same block — a back-run or a bundle — and has τ = 0 by construction. For CEX-triggered arbitrages we also record the number k of blocks sealed between the opening and the arbitrage and the two components of the overshoot of Section 3.2: the jump J = |p_pre/P_open − 1| − γ, where P_open is the price of the crossing trade, and the movement M = (|dev_pre| − γ) − J.

The response times identify the latency in three ways, each used in Section 6.9. Under P6 the quantile intercepts Q_q(τ) − q·Δt estimate ℓ. Because the arbitrage was not in the block before the one it landed in, its latency lies in the interval (t_{b−1} − t_open, t_b − t_open]; the nonparametric maximum-likelihood estimator of Turnbull (1976) for interval-censored data recovers the distribution of ℓ from these intervals without assuming anything about the phase of the block grid. And for on-chain-triggered arbitrages the trigger sits on the block grid, so k = ⌈ℓ/Δt⌉ and the share landing in the first block is the distribution function of ℓ at Δt. Because arbitrageurs may wait for a deviation somewhat larger than γ, we also compute response times to wider bands (1.5γ and 2γ) and for *sharp* openings — those in which the crossing trade itself carried the price at least half a band beyond the edge, so that t_open is unambiguous. The arbitrageur is identified in two steps. The swap event records only the *sender* — the contract that called the pool — and one operator may run several contracts and sign from many wallets; for every contract with at least twenty strict arbitrages in a window we therefore retrieve, from the transaction receipts, the externally owned account that signed each of its swaps (tx.from), its target and its gas. Two kinds of contract appear. A *public* contract — PancakeSwap's SmartRouter and Universal Router, aggregators, the routers of retail trading bots — is called by many unrelated accounts, most of them once: at least 30 distinct signing accounts with a median of at most two strict arbitrages each (the SmartRouter in the Fermi window: 501 accounts, median one). Every other contract is *private*, including the arbitrage bots that rotate a fixed pool of hot wallets, each of which signs tens to thousands of arbitrages (one contract signs from exactly 41 wallets, another from 50, a third from up to 189). Private contracts and the wallets that sign for them form a bipartite graph whose connected components are the *operators*; an arbitrage through a public contract is attributed to the operator of the account that sent it if that account also uses a private contract, to the account itself as an operator if it sent at least twenty such arbitrages in the window, and to anonymous flow otherwise. The response-time and component statistics of Section 6.9 use the arbitrages of private contracts — *bot flow* — because the trades that reach the pools through public routers from one-off accounts are not responses to the reference price at all: they are ordinary order flow that happens to trade in the correcting direction and to land inside the band. Their profile is unmistakable (Table D11): median size 600–1,100 USDT against 1,800–3,600 for the bots, median overshoot 0.04–0.07 bp against 0.4–0.7, half to three quarters of them same-block trades, and negative latency intercepts, since a trade that arrives at a random time in a pool whose band is crossed every few hundred milliseconds is always "shortly after" the last crossing. Router flow of both kinds is 6–23% of strict arbitrages across the three windows (one-off accounts 3–13%, persistent accounts 4–11%) and matters only in the two WBNB pools, where it is 19–50% of strict arbitrages in the Lorentz and Maxwell windows (1–4% in the ETH and BTCB pools, 7–11% at Fermi); Appendix D reports the statistics with and without it.

Across the three ±30-day windows the seven pools contain 10.3 million identified arbitrages, of which 6.5 million were sent by arbitrage contracts (1.7 million around Lorentz, 2.3 million around Maxwell, 2.6 million around Fermi) and the rest through public routers; the statistics that follow use the former, almost all of them in the four liquid pools. Among their strict arbitrages in the three core pools, CEX-triggered ones are 29–70% depending on pool and regime, continuations 20–41%, same-block back-runs 4–43% and on-chain-triggered ones 0.4–1.6%. The per-arbitrage quantities are aggregated to the same pool-hours as the other outcomes — the share of each trigger type, the mean overshoot of each type, and the mean J and M of the CEX-triggered ones — so that each component's fork effect can be estimated with the specification of Section 5; the hourly mean over all strict arbitrages, bot flow and router flow together, reproduces the overshoot series of Section 4.3 and the ±14-day estimates of Table B1 to the third decimal, and the component estimates of Section 6.9 are computed on bot flow. Appendix D validates the construction on simulated data with known latencies and reports the address-level statistics.

## 5. Empirical Strategy

### 5.1 Panel specification

For pool p and hour t we estimate

log y_pt = β·Post_t + δ·log σ_pt + X_pt'κ + α_p + η_h(t) + ε_pt,

where y is the outcome (strict overshoot, arbitrageur profit, LPs' gross loss, loss per arbitrage, number of arbitrages), Post is one after the fork, σ_pt is the hour's Binance realised volatility, η_h(t) are 24 hour-of-day fixed effects and α_p pool fixed effects. Three sets of controls X are used in turn: none (the *baseline*), the log of active liquidity L and log volume, and additionally a linear time trend in days. Standard errors are clustered by calendar day. Because the outcomes are in logs, β is approximately the proportional change; the predictions of Section 3 are θ for overshoot and profit and −s·(1 − √(Δt₁/Δt₀)) for the LPs' gross loss. We report estimates pooled over the three core pools (with pool fixed effects) and pool by pool, in ±14-day, ±30-day and ±7-day windows.

The controls address specific threats. Realised volatility enters every prediction and moved in different directions around the three forks. Liquidity and volume enter arbitrageur profit and LP loss mechanically (both scale with L) and absorb the activity cycles described below. The linear trend absorbs slow drifts within the window, at the cost of collinearity with Post in short windows.

### 5.2 Why the windows and controls have to be chosen carefully

None of the events is a clean step in an otherwise stationary environment (Figure A1). Around Lorentz, the Binance Alpha trading-rewards programme started in early May 2025, two weeks after the fork, on top of PancakeSwap's tokenomics overhaul and the launch of PancakeSwap Infinity: daily swaps in the WBNB/USDT 0.05% pool jumped from 50,000–110,000 to 140,000–310,000, volume in the ±14-day window was four times higher after the fork than before (2.7 times at ±30 days), gas used per second on the chain rose five-fold within two months, and the US tariff shock of 2–9 April raised volatility in the first half of the pre-window. Around Maxwell, the same programme was receding: the WBNB/USDT 0.05% pool traded 60,000–120,000 swaps a day in early June, 10,000–25,000 in late June and 25,000–45,000 in mid-July, so that volume in the ±30-day window was 27% lower after the fork than before in that pool and the share of its swaps classified as arbitrage rose mechanically from 8% to 21% as noise trading receded; volatility was 16–24% lower in the two weeks after Maxwell but 58% higher for BNB over the ±30-day window. Around Fermi, the crypto sell-off of 1–6 February 2026 falls in days 18–30 of the post-window: realised volatility in the ±30-day post-period is 1.7–1.8 times its pre-period level, whereas in the ±14-day window (which ends on 28 January) the ratio is 1.08–1.14. In addition, the overshoot residuals in the core pools drifted down during the two weeks before Fermi and before Lorentz, and up during the two weeks before Maxwell, for reasons we cannot attribute to volatility.

Activity waves of this size contaminate every per-volume statistic. After Lorentz the WBNB/USDT 0.05% pool's LP loss per hour rose from 361 to 682 USDT while its loss per unit of volume fell from 3.6 to 1.7 bp, because the added volume was uninformed; we therefore base the LP results on the loss in currency units with volume as a control, and treat per-volume net returns as descriptive.

We treat the choice of window and controls as something to be tested rather than assumed, with three devices. **Matched pairs** compare each post-fork hour with the same weekday and hour 14 days earlier and report the median ratio, a non-parametric check that does not rely on the functional form of the controls. A **regression-discontinuity-style** specification adds a time trend that is allowed to change slope at the fork, so that the jump at the fork is estimated separately from any pre-existing drift. And a **fake-fork placebo** re-estimates every specification on the pre-period only, with a fictitious fork at its midpoint: a specification that "detects" an effect where there was none is contaminated by the ambient trend and is not used to state a preferred estimate.

### 5.3 What the placebo tests select

The placebo results (Table 9, Section 6.7) are different for the three events, and the same under either reference price. For Lorentz, only the ±30-day specification with the trend (and the ±30-day RD jump, whose pre-trend is estimated) is clean; every ±14-day specification detects a spurious −0.10 to −0.16 for the overshoot. For Maxwell, the ±14-day window is clean for the overshoot under every set of controls (+0.02, +0.03, −0.02), as are the two ±30-day specifications without a trend (−0.00, −0.03), while the ±30-day trend specification detects −0.07 and the ±30-day baselines detect −0.34 for profit and −0.15 for LP loss — the Alpha cycle. For Fermi, the ±14-day specification with liquidity and volume (−0.06), the ±30-day trend specification (−0.06) and the ±30-day RD jump are clean, while the ±30-day baseline detects −0.11, the ±14-day baseline −0.10, and the ±14-day trend specification, though its placebo is only +0.11 (s.e. 0.07), cannot separate the fork-day jump from a pre-trend of −1.2% per day (Section 6.1). We accordingly report the full grid of specifications and windows for all three forks (Tables 4 and 6, Figure 2) and describe as *placebo-clean* the specifications just listed.

### 6.2 What the reference price does to these estimates

Every estimate above uses the trade reference. Table C1 places the candle-reference estimates next to them for the placebo-clean specifications, and Table C2 measures the wedge between the two directly: the log ratio of the strict overshoot computed with the trade reference to the same hour's overshoot computed with the candle reference, in the same pool-hours, regressed on Post, log σ, hour and pool effects, with a trend allowed to change slope at the fork.

**Figure 4. The wedge between the two references, day by day.** Daily mean of log(strict overshoot with the trade reference / with the candle reference) in the three core pools (black, left axis) and the daily mean reference gap |candle/trade − 1| (blue, right axis), ±30 days around each fork.

![Figure 4](figures_v41/fig6_wedge.png)

Three facts stand out. First, the candle reference overstates the effect of the two sub-second forks by about half: the Maxwell and Fermi overshoot effects are 0.08–0.15 log points larger with the candle than with the trade reference in every placebo-clean specification, the profit effects 0.24–0.34 larger, and the count of arbitrages, which is flat or rising with the trade reference, appears to fall by 9–13% after Fermi with the candle. With the candle reference the two forks looked like 58–101% and 76–87% of the √Δt law, in line with Lorentz; with the trade reference they are 28–59% and 44–51%. Second, the wedge is a step at the fork and nothing else: it is flat before each fork (pre-fork slopes of at most 0.0003 per day in absolute value in the ±30-day windows), jumps on the fork day by 0.03 at Lorentz, 0.13 at Maxwell and 0.08 at Fermi, and is flat afterwards (Figure 4), while volatility does not jump and the reference gap jumps only at Lorentz, where fractional-second timestamps first appear. Controlling for the hour's reference gap reduces the Maxwell and Fermi jumps only from 0.128 to 0.121 and from 0.079 to 0.067: the bias is not that the candle is noisier after the fork, it is that the same staleness now falls on the wrong side of more arbitrage trades. Third, Lorentz behaves exactly as the mechanism of Section 4.2 requires. Before it the two references coincide, the wedge is identically zero and the reference gap is zero; at the fork the half-second timestamps appear, the reference gap jumps to 0.15 bp, and the wedge jumps by 0.030 (0.007) — an amount that the reference-gap control absorbs entirely (0.011, s.e. 0.009). The ordering of the three jumps, Lorentz ≪ Fermi ≲ Maxwell, is the ordering of the change in the share of arbitrage trades whose triggering price move postdates the candle close (Section 4.2). The number of strict arbitrages identified is almost the same under the two references (trade-to-candle ratios of 0.86–1.26 across the six regimes, against 1.0–1.5 for all identified arbitrages), so the wedge in the strict overshoot is a mismeasurement of largely the same trades rather than a change in which trades are counted.

Two things follow. The candle-based results of the earlier version of this paper — and, we suspect, of any study that aligns sub-second on-chain trades to second-level CEX candles — contain an artefact of the block interval itself. And the artefact is largest exactly where the interesting economics is: at the transition from 1.5 s to sub-second blocks, where the candle's staleness becomes comparable to the block interval. The rest of the paper uses the trade reference; Appendix C keeps the candle-reference tables for comparison.

### 6.3 Arbitrageurs' profits fall by 8–22% (P2)

Arbitrageur profit is a noisier outcome — it is the product of the overshoot, the trade size and the number of trades, and its hourly sums are dominated by a few large episodes — and it is the outcome most sensitive to the reference price, because it values each arbitrage's position at the reference (Table 6, first column of each block). After Lorentz, the placebo-clean specifications give −0.244 (0.062) and −0.245 (0.056), 70% of the predicted −0.347; the baseline without liquidity and volume controls gives +0.24, because the activity wave raised the number of profitable opportunities. After Maxwell, the ±14-day specifications with a trend or the RD design give −0.212 (0.111) and −0.218 (0.082), while those without a trend give +0.05 and +0.08: the Alpha cycle was receding through the window, and a specification that does not allow for it attributes the recovery of profits in July to the fork. The profit placebos of this window are contaminated in both directions (+0.16 without a trend, −0.15 with it; Table 9), so the Maxwell profit effect is the least well identified of the three: its sign is robust across the trend and RD designs, its size is not. The ±30-day specifications without a trend, −0.31 and −0.22, are placebo-contaminated for this outcome as well (Table 9). After Fermi, the one placebo-clean specification whose profit placebo is also clean, ±14 days with liquidity and volume, gives −0.088 (0.048) against a prediction of −0.255; the ±30-day trend and RD specifications give −0.133 (0.067) and −0.137 (0.065), but their profit placebos detect −0.30. Pool by pool (Table B1), profits fell in all four liquid pools after Lorentz (−0.11 to −0.52), in the BTCB pool after Maxwell (−0.32) and in the 1 bp pool after Fermi (−0.56); they rose in the WBNB 5 bp pool after Maxwell (+0.32) and are insignificant elsewhere, apart from the CAKE/WBNB pool after Lorentz (−0.29) and the USDC/USDT placebo pool after Fermi, whose volume rose more than tenfold after the fork. The placebo tests detect spurious profit "effects" of up to 0.43 in absolute value in some specifications, so we read the profit results as confirming the sign of P2 and an order of magnitude — 8–22% per fork — that is smaller than the overshoot prediction, as the overshoot estimates themselves are.

### 6.6 The effect is multiplicative within a fork (P4)

The model's predictions are in logs and do not depend on σ, γ or L. Table B3 tests this in the ±14-day windows with the liquidity, volume and trend specification by interacting Post with the demeaned log volatility, with an indicator for above-median-volatility hours, and with an indicator for the Asian trading session (01:00–08:00 UTC). For Maxwell none of the interactions is distinguishable from zero (+0.07, +0.02 and +0.04; p between 0.17 and 0.70) and the main effect keeps its size; for Fermi the volatility-level and session interactions are zero (−0.04 and +0.02) and the high-volatility indicator is negative at the 5% margin (−0.10, p = 0.05), which we do not read as evidence against P4 given the number of tests. For Lorentz the interaction with log volatility is positive and significant (+0.13, p = 0.01): the effect is smaller in high-volatility hours. Because the high-volatility hours after Lorentz are concentrated in the second half of May, at the peak of the activity wave, we read this as heterogeneity in activity rather than in volatility, and note that the ±14-day window in which it is estimated fails the placebo test for this fork.

Across pools the multiplicative prediction holds at Lorentz and holds loosely at the two later forks. The three core pools' pre-fork exposures σ√Δt/γ span a factor of 1.5–3 within each fork (Table 2), and their coefficients in the ±14-day liquidity-and-volume specification are −0.24, −0.33 and −0.39 (Lorentz), −0.18, −0.11 and −0.09 (Maxwell) and −0.14, −0.04 and −0.11 (Fermi), with no monotonic relation to exposure. The cleanest test is the 1 bp pool at 3-second blocks: with an exposure of 0.92 — the price drifts almost a full band within one block — and an overshoot of 0.7 bp, far above the reference-price noise, its coefficient after Lorentz is −0.36 (0.03) in the same specification and −0.29 (0.05) in the placebo-clean ±30-day trend specification, the same as the 5 bp pools with exposures of 0.18–0.42. At the two sub-second forks the 1 bp pool's band is of the order of the reference noise (mean gap 0.06–0.44 bp, RMS 0.24–1.24 bp), its overshoot is a few tenths of a basis point, and its coefficients (+0.01 after Maxwell, −0.14 after Fermi) are not a clean test. The CAKE/WBNB and 1% pools, where strict arbitrages are rare, give imprecise estimates (standard errors of 0.09–0.33) that do not differ from zero at the 5% level.

### 6.8 Aligning the reference to the arbitrageur's information: a 250 ms lag

The trade reference is aligned to the block timestamp, but the arbitrageur observed the CEX price before the block was produced — by the time it took to build and broadcast the transaction and to wait for inclusion, at most one block interval. If that delay is δ, the deviation the arbitrageur acted on is the pool price against the CEX price at t − δ, and a reference at t measures it with an error of order σ√δ that, being drawn after the decision, is symmetric around zero but inflates the absolute deviation and does so more at longer intervals, where δ is larger. We re-estimated every panel with the reference taken 250 ms before the block timestamp — roughly the middle of the post-fork interval at Fermi and a third of it at Maxwell — using the same aggregate-trade data (Appendix C reports the full comparison).

Moving the reference 250 ms earlier changes the estimates in one direction and by a bounded amount. The overshoot effects become larger by 0.01–0.04 log points at both forks (Fermi 44–51% → 52–63% of the prediction, Maxwell 28–59% → 31–71%), the profit effects by 0.04–0.15 (Fermi's, the most sensitive outcome, from 8–13% to 19–25%), while the LP loss (changes of at most 0.015), the σ elasticity (0.43–0.59 under either alignment) and the placebo results are unchanged in sign and, with one exception, in significance (the Fermi ±14-day trend overshoot placebo rises from +0.11 to +0.13 and becomes significant at 5%; that specification is not among the placebo-clean ones). Hour by hour, the lag-250 overshoot is 2–6% below the lag-0 one at Fermi and 0–1% at Maxwell, and the gap widens by 0.02–0.05 at the fork: a fixed lag is a larger fraction of a shorter block, so the lagged reference sits closer to the arbitrageur's decision after the fork than before, and removes more post-decision drift from the post-period measurement. Because the arbitrageur's information lag presumably scales with the block interval, a lag proportional to Δt would give estimates between the two columns, and we read them as bounds. Within those bounds every conclusion of Sections 6.1 and 7.2 holds: the pooled elasticity across the three forks is 0.19–0.38 with the lagged estimates against 0.15–0.38 without (Appendix Table C6), equality across forks is rejected at the 10% level in eight of eleven specifications (at 5% in seven), and the latency-floor model fits the RD-type estimates with ℓ = 0.46 s (0.20–0.88; χ² = 0.11 on two degrees of freedom) and rejects ℓ = 0 (χ² = 18.5 on three), predicting that a further halving to 0.225 s would reduce the overshoot by 9–13%.

### 6.9 Response times and the anatomy of the overshoot: what the diminishing returns are made of (P6, P7)

**Response times and latency.** Table 10 summarises the response times of CEX-triggered arbitrages by arbitrage contracts in the three core pools across the six block-interval regimes (Table D1 gives the four liquid pools regime by regime; Appendix D shows what including the flow that reaches the pools through public routers does to the estimates); Figure 5 plots the latency intercepts. At sub-second block times the picture is the one P6 describes. In the Fermi window the intercepts of the tenth percentile and of the median differ by less than a tenth of a second and move by less than 50 ms at the fork (WBNB 125/187 → 132/203 ms, ETH 206/271 → 162/237 ms, BTCB 133/192 → 109/160 ms), the Turnbull estimator places the median latency at 160–230 ms, and the median response time falls by 134, 184 and 182 ms in the three pools against the 150 ms by which the expected wait for a block fell (Table D2); at Maxwell the corresponding changes are −420, −504 and −505 ms against −375, and the intercepts are 0.20–0.47 s. Openings created by a sharp jump of the reference price, whose timing is unambiguous, give intercepts of 0.08–0.40 s in every one of the six regimes. On-chain-triggered arbitrages, which use only block timestamps, tell the same story from the other side: the share landing in the first block, which is the distribution function of the latency at Δt, is 0.80–0.91 whether Δt is 3 s or 0.45 s, so four fifths to nine tenths of the latency distribution lies below 0.45 s and the remainder is a tail that does not respond to the block interval at all (small deviations that arbitrageurs wait to grow). Wider bands give intercepts near zero or negative: arbitrageurs act at the band edge, not beyond it. At 3-second blocks, in April 2025, arbitrageurs were slower and more heterogeneous — median intercepts of 0.56 s (WBNB), 0.75 s (BTCB) and 1.4 s (ETH) — and they became faster within the Lorentz window itself (latency-change components of −0.06 to −0.11 in three pools in Table D2; +0.09 in BTCB, whose mean response time is held up by a long tail) and kept doing so through the year: the same operators appear in every pool and regime, and the tenth-percentile intercept of the contract that was the largest ETH-pool arbitrageur before Lorentz fell from 954 ms then to 151 ms after Fermi (Table D5). As a fixed latency implies, the share of CEX-triggered arbitrages that land in the first block after the opening falls at both sub-second forks in every pool — from 0.68–0.85 to 0.54–0.64 at Maxwell and from 0.62–0.73 to 0.49–0.63 at Fermi (Figure D1) — so that at 0.45-second blocks 37–51% of arbitrages miss the first block; at Lorentz it fell in the ETH and BTCB pools and rose in the two WBNB pools, whose arbitrageurs became faster within the window.

**What the response times imply for the fork effects.** If the overshoot were σ√τ, the fork effect would be log(E[√τ]_post / E[√τ]_pre); Table D2 computes it from the response times alone and places it next to the estimated overshoot effect of each pool (Table B1). The response-time-implied effects track the estimates across the twelve pool-forks (correlation 0.67, weighted slope 1.06 with s.e. 0.38; Figure D2) and coincide with them at Lorentz — −0.35, −0.33, −0.17 and −0.37 against −0.39, −0.33, −0.24 and −0.36 — but at the two sub-second forks they are systematically larger than the estimates — −0.16 to −0.24 against −0.04 to −0.18 in the 5 bp pools, and −0.29 against +0.01 in the 1 bp pool at Maxwell: the latency accounts for part of the sub-second shortfall, not for all of it. Holding the pre-fork latency distribution fixed and changing only Δt reproduces the post-fork response-time distribution almost exactly at Maxwell and Fermi (latency-change components between −0.03 and +0.09), whereas at Lorentz the arbitrageurs also became faster. Expressed in the convention of Section 3.2 and Table 12, the latency that the response times measure is 0.12–0.36 s at Fermi and 0.22–0.89 s at Maxwell — consistent with the χ² fit at Maxwell, at or below the lower end of its confidence interval at Fermi.

**The components of the overshoot.** Table 11 estimates the fork effect on each component of the strict overshoot with the specification of Table 4 (core pools pooled, pool fixed effects), Table D3 does so pool by pool together with the pre-fork weight of each component, and Figure 6 plots the pool-level estimates. Three results. First, the accumulated-movement component M falls at every fork, by the √Δt law or more at Lorentz and Fermi and by about half of it at Maxwell: −0.46 (0.04), −0.19 (0.04) and −0.32 (0.04) in the ±14-day windows against −0.35, −0.35 and −0.26, with −0.46, −0.24 and −0.32 at ±7 days and −0.56, −0.17 and −0.32 at ±30 days; pool by pool the estimates are −0.28 to −0.78, −0.10 to −0.49 and −0.23 to −0.47, and the fake-fork placebos for this component are −0.07, +0.04 and −0.09. That M falls by more than the response times imply is what a fixed latency produces once the selection of the arbitrages that actually occur is taken into account — an opportunity is only closed if the price stays outside the band until the block, and that condition binds more at longer response times (Appendix D reproduces the pattern in a simulation with a fixed latency). Second, the components that the model says should not respond respond little. The crossing-jump component J changes by −0.02 (0.03), +0.04 (0.03) and −0.06 (0.02) at the three forks (−0.07 to +0.04 across windows), and the overshoot of same-block back-runs by +0.06 (0.04) at Maxwell and −0.11 (0.05) at Fermi (pool by pool, −0.30 to +0.27): J moves by a quarter of the law at most, the back-runs by nothing at Maxwell and by two fifths of the law at Fermi, where M moves by more than the law; continuations fall by −0.37, −0.21 and −0.18, by less than M at Lorentz and Fermi and by as much at Maxwell. The jump component also has among the lowest elasticities to volatility — 0.04–0.15, against 0.43–0.54 for M (Table D3, lower panel) — as a quantity set by the discreteness of the reference price rather than by its diffusion should. Third, the weighted sum of the components' effects, with each pool's pre-fork weights, reproduces the total: −0.30 against a total of −0.27 at Lorentz, −0.12 against −0.12 at Maxwell and −0.15 against −0.12 at Fermi (means over the four liquid pools). The weights are what makes the returns diminish. In the pre-fork fortnight the movement component M is 28% of the strict overshoot (8–54% across the twelve pool-forks), the jump 19%, continuations 34% and same-block back-runs 18%; the two components that do not respond together rise from 32% of the overshoot at Lorentz to 35% at Maxwell and 44% at Fermi, because each fork removes part of M and none of the rest. At Fermi, w_M times the √Δt law is −0.06, w_M times the estimated effect on M is −0.08, and the continuations add about −0.05 — the −0.11 to −0.14 that the aggregate estimates show (−0.14 on bot flow, −0.11 to −0.13 with all flow). ## 7. Discussion

### 7.1 Why the LPs' cost does not fall

The result that organises the paper is the contrast between the arbitrageurs' rent, which falls at every fork, and the LPs' gross loss, which does not. It follows from the fee band. The pool loses relative to the external price whenever it executes at a stale price, and the stale price can be up to γ away from the external price without any arbitrageur having an incentive to correct it. The part of the staleness that depends on the block interval is the overshoot beyond the band — 0.3–2.8 bp in our pools against a band of 5 bp — and only this part shrinks when blocks get faster. Halving the block interval removed about 25% of the overshoot at 3-second blocks and 9–19% below 1.5 s; the arbitrageurs' profit fell by about the same fraction; and because that profit is a quarter to three-quarters of the gross loss at 3-second blocks and a fifth to two-fifths at sub-second blocks, the LPs' loss was predicted to fall by 6–16% at Lorentz and by 2–8% at the later forks, and fell by 6% and by nothing. The rest of the LPs' loss is the price of allowing the pool price to sit anywhere inside the band, and no block-time change will remove it; only a narrower band (a lower fee, at the cost of fee income), an oracle-updated price, or a mechanism that makes arbitrageurs pay for the right to correct the price (as in the auction-managed AMM of Adams et al., 2026) would.

Table 7 says the same thing in levels. In the 5 bp pools for ETH and BTCB, the LPs' loss marked to the contemporaneous CEX price is 79–120% of their fee income in every one of the six block-interval regimes; in the WBNB 5 bp pool it is 84–100% whenever the pool is not flooded with rewards-driven volume. This is the zero-profit condition of a competitive LP in the LVR-with-fees model, and it holds at 3-second blocks and at 0.45-second blocks alike. Read this way, the sub-second block roadmaps of BNB Chain and of layer-2 sequencers are good for price efficiency and bad for arbitrageurs, but of second-order importance for the passive LP — and of diminishing importance as the interval shrinks, both because the arbitrageurs' share of the loss shrinks with it and because, as the next subsection shows, the overshoot itself stops responding.

The pure √Δt law is rejected by every estimate set (χ² of 23 to 178 on three degrees of freedom). The RD-type estimates are fitted almost exactly by a latency of 0.59 s (95% interval 0.27–1.13 s; χ² = 0.55 on two degrees of freedom); the estimate set that uses the Maxwell specification without a trend, in which the Maxwell effect (−0.11) is smaller than the Fermi effect (−0.13), and the set that pools each fork's specifications are fitted less well by a monotone floor (χ² p = 0.01 and 0.00) and yield ℓ ≈ 1.0–1.1 s as a compromise. The fitted model says that halving the block interval again, from 0.45 s to 0.225 s, would reduce the overshoot at arbitrage by 7–12%, not by the 29% of the √Δt law, and that the overshoot at an infinitely fast chain would still be 75–85% of its level at 0.45 s. On bot flow (sets D–F, the same choices of specification with router flow excluded) the fitted latency is 1.0–1.05 s (95% intervals 0.5–1.9 s) whichever set is used, the RD-type set is again fitted well (χ² = 1.8, p = 0.40), the law is still rejected (χ² ≥ 37), and the forward prediction is the same: −0.08 for the next halving and a floor of 83–84% of the 0.45-second level.

Section 6.9 says what this fitted ℓ is. Translated into a response latency (a fixed delay of ℓ_t before a wait uniform over the block interval gives an effective ℓ of about 2.2–2.4·ℓ_t in the convention of the fit), 0.59 s corresponds to ℓ_t ≈ 0.25 s (0.11–0.52 s) and the 1.0 s of the bot-flow fit to ℓ_t ≈ 0.45 s, and the response times measure ℓ_t at 0.20–0.47 s at Maxwell and 0.11–0.27 s at Fermi. The latency is real, of the order the fit requires at Maxwell, and smaller than it at Fermi; and it is not fixed over the year — the arbitrageurs who took a second to respond in April 2025 took a fifth of a second in January 2026. What the fitted ℓ absorbs on top of it is the composition of the overshoot. In the 5 bp pools the response times alone would predict fork effects of −0.16 to −0.24 at the two sub-second forks; the estimates are −0.04 to −0.18 because only a quarter of the strict overshoot is the movement accumulated during the response time, while the jump of the reference price that opens the opportunity and the overshoots of same-block back-runs, 32–44% of the total, respond little to the clock and the continuations respond partly. A single-parameter latency floor fitted to aggregate effects is therefore an *effective* latency: it summarises, in one number, a delay of a quarter of a second and a floor of a different kind — the discreteness of the reference price and of the on-chain flow that creates arbitrage opportunities. The arbitrageurs' side of the latency floor is visible in how they organise: at 0.45-second blocks the fastest operators sign from pools of 41–189 wallets to keep several transactions in flight, and the operators with more wallets are the faster ones — a relation absent at 1.5-second blocks — while the gas premia for position within the block that were common at 3-second blocks have shrunk to fractions of a gwei (Section 6.9).

The two floors give the same forward prediction by different routes. Weighting the √Δt law by the movement component's share at Fermi (0.25) gives −0.09 for a further halving from 0.45 s to 0.225 s; adding the partial response of continuations at the rate observed at Fermi gives about −0.15; the latency fit gives −0.08 to −0.12. For the limit, the components that respond little are 44% of the overshoot in the fortnight before Fermi in the four liquid pools (40–47% across pools; 37% on average over the twelve pool-forks, 17–54%) and 41% in the fortnight after it, when continuations are a further 36%. If a continuation's overshoot is a residual that does not respond plus a drift that scales with √Δt, its response at Fermi (−0.18) implies that about three tenths of it is residual, so the decomposition puts the overshoot at an infinitely fast chain at 0.41 + 0.36 × 0.3 ≈ 0.52 of its 0.45-second level — about one half (0.47–0.63 with the ±30-day and ±7-day continuation estimates; 0.41 if continuations vanished with the block interval, 0.77 if none of them responded) — against the three quarters to 85% of the latency fit. Either way, the block interval has done most of what it can do for the mispricing at which arbitrageurs trade.

The discreteness floor is not a measurement artefact of the kind discussed in Section 4.2. The trade reference has a noise floor — the minimum price increment, bid–ask bounce, the alignment of two clocks — of perhaps 0.1–0.3 bp, and a floor of that size added in quadrature to a true overshoot of 0.5–0.7 bp would also compress a true log change of −0.255 to about −0.18. Three observations weigh against measurement noise as the explanation. The price increment is largest for BNB (0.1–0.2 bp) and negligible for BTC, so it predicts the smallest Fermi effect in the WBNB pool and the largest in BTCB, whereas the estimates are −0.14 for BTCB, −0.11 for WBNB and −0.04 for ETH; the all-arbitrage overshoot, two to three times larger than the strict one and correspondingly less affected by a floor, shows smaller rather than larger effects; and the mean jump component J of strict CEX-triggered arbitrages is 0.21–0.50 bp in the 5 bp pools across the forks, a component of the price path rather than of our measurement — the arbitrageur cannot trade before the reference price has jumped, whatever the block interval. Section 6.8 adds that a reference lagged by 250 ms, which changes the alignment of the reference to the arbitrageur's information but not the price increment, raises the overshoot estimates by 0.01–0.04 and lowers the fitted ℓ to 0.46 s (0.20–0.88): alignment accounts for a seventh to a quarter of the gap to the law at Fermi, the arbitrageurs' latency for a further part, and the composition of the overshoot for the rest.

### 7.3 The elasticity of the overshoot to volatility is one half, not one

In every event, at hourly frequency, before and after the fork and under either reference price, the coefficient of log overshoot on log σ in the specifications with only volatility and hour effects lies between 0.34 and 0.59 for the pooled core pools and between 0.19 and 0.76 pool by pool (Appendix Table A2), never near the model's 1. The trade reference lowers it by at most 0.16 relative to the candle, and by nothing in most cells, so reference noise explains little of the shortfall. Two explanations are consistent with the rest of the evidence. One is the latency floor itself: with an effective interval Δt + ℓ, the hourly overshoot still scales with σ, but the arbitrageurs who act during a high-volatility hour face a stronger incentive to shorten their own ℓ, compressing the overshoot relative to σ√(Δt + ℓ). The other is that competition responds to volatility more generally: when σ is high, more arbitrageurs are active and each mispricing is corrected sooner in wall-clock time, whereas a permanent, pre-announced change in Δt does not trigger the same reallocation within a few weeks. The decomposition of Section 6.9 adds a third, mechanical, explanation: the components of the overshoot have different elasticities to volatility — 0.43–0.54 for the accumulated movement, 0.04–0.15 for the crossing jump, whose size is set by the discreteness of trades on the reference exchange — so the aggregate elasticity is a weighted average that lies below the elasticity of the part the model describes. All three have the same implication for the design: the volatility control absorbs only about half of a shift in σ, so credible estimates require windows in which σ is stable, which is why the placebo tests are used to select windows and why the ±30-day Fermi window, in which volatility doubled, is not used for the profit and LP results without a trend.

### 7.4 Limitations

The reference price is now the last Binance trade before the block timestamp at millisecond resolution, which removes the staleness bias but not the noise floor discussed above, and it is aligned to the block timestamp rather than to the moment at which the arbitrageur observed the CEX price; the 250 ms lag of Section 6.8 brackets that choice with a single fixed lag, whereas the arbitrageur's information lag presumably scales with the block interval, so the lag-0 and lag-250 estimates should be read as bounds rather than as a point. Arbitrage identification is heuristic and price-based; it captures CEX–DEX arbitrage but does not separate DEX–DEX arbitrage or informed non-arbitrage flow. The forks bundled gas-limit and finality changes; none affected congestion or pricing in our windows, but they cannot be separated from the block-time change. The Lorentz window is the least clean of the three, and only one specification survives its placebo test; the Maxwell window contains a pre-trend that makes its estimate a range rather than a point. The events are three, on one chain and one protocol, and every window contains confounders that we handle with controls, placebos and matched pairs rather than with a control group: a cross-chain control (Uniswap v3 on Ethereum, PancakeSwap v3 on Arbitrum) would add a difference-in-differences design, at the cost of comparability. The opening time of an opportunity is reconstructed from the reference exchange's trade record and the pool's own trades; it is exact for on-chain triggers and for sharp openings, and a last-crossing convention for gradual ones, which biases the response time downward when the price hovers at the band edge (as it does in the 1 bp pool, whose intercepts are reliable only for sharp openings). Response times conditional on an arbitrage having occurred are a selected sample — slow responses are more often pre-empted by the price returning into the band — which compresses the upper tail of the estimated latency distribution; the simulation of Appendix D reproduces this selection and the component pattern of Section 6.9 with a fixed latency and no behavioural change. Arbitrageurs are identified by their contracts and by the accounts that sign for them; the flow that reaches the pools through public routers from one-off accounts — 6–23% of strict arbitrages, concentrated in the WBNB pools — cannot be attributed to an operator and is excluded from the response-time and component statistics as ordinary order flow, which it resembles in every observable respect; a bot that rotated a fresh account for every trade would be excluded with it, and the aggregate estimates of Sections 6.1–6.7, which include this flow, describe the mispricing that LPs face rather than the bots' rents alone (Table B5 gives the overshoot estimates without it). Finally, our LP loss is a pool-level, mark-to-CEX proxy in the spirit of Fritsch and Canidio (2024); a position-level reconstruction from Mint and Burn events (Heimbach, Schertenleib and Wattenhofer, 2022) would allow statements about LPs' realised returns and range choices.

## 8. Related Work

**AMM theory and LVR.** Milionis et al. (2022) define loss-versus-rebalancing and show it equals ½σ² times a curvature term of the pool; Milionis, Moallemi and Roughgarden (2025) add fees and Poisson block arrivals and derive the fast-block regime we use, in which arbitrage profit equals LVR scaled by the probability that a block is profitable; Capponi and Jia (2025) model LPs' adverse selection by arbitrageurs and the role of gas costs; Park (2023) shows that constant-function pricing exposes LPs to arbitrage by construction; Hasbrouck, Rivera and Saleh (2026a) build an equilibrium model of concentrated liquidity; Cartea, Drissi and Monga (2024) and Singh et al. (2025) characterise LVR as a predictable loss and as an option-financing cost. Design responses include batch trading (Canidio and Fritsch, 2023), auction-managed AMMs (Adams et al., 2026) and optimal fees (Campbell et al., 2025; Hasbrouck, Rivera and Saleh, 2026b); Xu et al. (2023) survey the design space. Our contribution is to measure, in a setting where Δt changed exogenously three times, which of these quantities actually move and by how much, and to show that the block-interval elasticity of arbitrage rents flattens below about one second.

**Empirical DEX market quality.** Lehar and Parlour (2025) document stable liquidity and few durable arbitrage opportunities on Uniswap, and Lehar, Parlour and Zoican (2024) show how liquidity fragments across fee tiers, which is why our core sample holds the fee tier fixed; Barbon and Ranaldo (2026) and Alexander et al. (2025) compare price efficiency and price discovery across CEX and DEX; Capponi, Jia and Yu (2026) study how on-chain frictions, including block time, shape informed trading and price discovery; Fritsch and Canidio (2024) measure arbitrage losses on Uniswap with CEX prices and simulate the effect of shorter blocks; Heimbach, Schertenleib and Wattenhofer (2022) and Urusov et al. (2026) study concentrated-liquidity LPs' returns. Di Nosse et al. (2025) note that PancakeSwap has the largest volume among DEXs but has been studied little because of data availability; our data pipeline, which reconstructs sub-second timestamps from sparse headers, aligns swaps to individual CEX trades and uses free public sources only, is one answer. The reference-price result of Section 6.2 bears on all of these studies: at sub-second block times, a CEX reference at second or minute resolution understates adverse selection and biases comparisons across block-time regimes.

**Speed and market design.** The idea that the rent from discrete trading opportunities accrues to the fastest trader and can be reduced by changing the market's clock is central to the equity-market debate on frequent batch auctions (Budish, Cramton and Shim, 2015). Block time is the crypto analogue of the batch interval, with the difference that here the clock was changed by the venue itself, three times, and that the transfers can be observed on a public ledger; the latency floor we estimate is the analogue of the finding that speed races persist at any batch length shorter than participants' own latency. Our address-level evidence is in the same spirit: a handful of operators, the largest of them one contract signing from a fixed pool of wallets, execute most CEX–DEX arbitrage on these pools; the main ones shortened their latency between April 2025 and January 2026 — from about a second to 0.15 s in the ETH pool, from 0.44 s to 0.06 s in the BTCB pool for the largest contract overall — while the chain's clock was shortened nearly seven-fold, each fork moved their response times by roughly the mechanical amount, with the arbitrageurs' own speed-up concentrated in the Lorentz and Maxwell windows, and at 0.45-second blocks the fastest of them sign from pools of 41–189 wallets to keep several transactions in flight — the on-chain form of the co-location race.

**MEV and arbitrage measurement.** Zhou et al. (2021) and Qin et al. (2022) quantify sandwich attacks and arbitrage on Ethereum; Öz et al. (2025) study cross-chain arbitrage, in which BNB Chain is a frequent leg; He, Yang and Zhou (2025) model gas competition among arbitrageurs and validate it on Binance–Uniswap data. Our identification of arbitrage by price deviation follows this literature; the finding that the classification depends on the resolution of the reference price in a way that co-moves with block time is, to our knowledge, new.

## 9. Conclusion

Three pre-announced reductions of the block interval on BNB Chain, on the same pools and with the same participants, show what the discreteness of block production is worth to whom, and where its value runs out. The mispricing that arbitrageurs trade on fell with the block interval at every step of the roadmap from 3 s to 0.45 s, but by a diminishing fraction of the √Δt law: 84% at the halving from 3 s — 60% on the trades of arbitrage contracts alone — and roughly a third to a half at the two reductions below 1.5 s. The elasticities differ across the forks in most specifications. Reconstructing when each of 6.5 million arbitrage opportunities opened shows why: arbitrageurs respond with a fixed latency of a tenth to a third of a second, and the part of the mispricing that accumulates while they wait for a block falls at every fork, by the √Δt law at two of the three; but that part is only a quarter to a third of the mispricing at which arbitrageurs trade, the rest being the jump of the reference price that opens the opportunity and the overshoots of arbitrages triggered by other trades in the pool, and these respond little to the chain's clock. The returns to faster blocks diminish because the responsive part shrinks and the unresponsive parts remain; a further halving would buy about a tenth of the remaining mispricing. Arbitrageurs' profits fell by 8–22% per fork and mispricing episodes shortened by 20–35%. The liquidity providers' gross cost of adverse selection fell by 6% at 3-second blocks and not at all below, and in the ETH and BTCB 5 bp pools it equals their fee income in every block-interval regime: the block interval is a lever over price efficiency and over the distribution of arbitrage rents, a weak and weakening lever over the cost of providing liquidity, which is set by the fee band, by the discreteness of the reference price and by the arbitrageurs' latency rather than by the chain's clock. A 1-second reference price, the standard in this literature, would have told a different and wrong story about the two sub-second forks — larger effects, a common elasticity, no floor — because its staleness co-moves with the block interval; measuring sub-second markets requires sub-second references.

## Appendix A. Data validation and the construction of the reference prices

**Timestamps.** Parlia sets block.timestamp to parent + nominal interval unless the chain is behind wall-clock time, in which case the block is stamped later, never earlier; since Lorentz the millisecond part is carried in the header's `mixHash` field, and before Lorentz blocks were stamped in whole seconds on a 3-second schedule. On 278,000 consecutive headers fetched during the pilot, 99.94% of adjacent pairs were exactly at the nominal spacing. The reconstruction fetches anchors every ten minutes, checks each span's elapsed time against the nominal one, and bisects mismatching spans; it reproduces every fetched header exactly. Where the log provider returned block timestamps (the 2.33 million swaps of the WBNB/USDT 0.05% pool in the Maxwell sample, for which the log provider returned block timestamps; all swap blocks in the Lorentz and Fermi samples via HyperSync), they agree with the reconstruction without exception.

**Swap logs.** The Lorentz and Fermi samples were retrieved from Envio HyperSync. On a reference slice of 20,000 blocks (52,193,176–52,213,175, 6,510 swaps in WBNB/USDT 0.05%) HyperSync and `eth_getLogs` return identical records (block number, log index, transaction hash, topics and data), and HyperSync's block timestamps agree with the reconstructed table for all 6,510 swaps.

**Reference prices.** Both references come from Binance's public data archive. The candle reference is the close of the last completed 1-second candle before the block timestamp. The trade reference is built from the daily aggregate-trade files (microsecond timestamps): each day's trades are collapsed to the last price of every millisecond and stored; for a swap with block timestamp t the reference is the price of the last trade at or before t, found by a backward search over the day containing t (and the previous day if needed), and the reference used for mark-outs at t + 5 s and t + 30 s is found in the same way. For the CAKE/WBNB pool the reference is the ratio of the CAKEUSDT and BNBUSDT references. Days with missing files are absent from the panel rather than interpolated. For every swap we keep both references, and the per-swap ratio candle/trade is the reference gap reported in Tables 7 and C2; its mean absolute value is zero before Lorentz (whole-second timestamps) and 0.06–1.81 bp afterwards in the core pools, rising with volatility.

## Appendix D. Opening times: construction, validation and address-level statistics

**Construction.** For each arbitrage the search runs backward from the block over the millisecond trade series of the reference (two days of trades in memory at a time), looking for the last trade whose price lies inside the band [p_pre/(1+γ), p_pre/(1−γ)] after the previous swap of the pool; the opening is the first trade after it. If no trade inside the band is found after the previous swap, the trigger is on chain: same-block if the previous swap is in the same block, on-chain-triggered if the previous swap moved the price out of the band from inside it, and a continuation otherwise; if none is found within 120 s of the block the opportunity is older than that and the arbitrage is excluded from the response-time statistics (well under 1% of arbitrages in the liquid pools). The number of blocks k between the opening and the arbitrage, the lower bound t_{b−1} − t_open of the latency interval and the components J and M follow from the block-timestamp table. The Turnbull estimator is run on a 10 ms grid up to 10 s. Response times below 100 ms — the diagnostic for clock offsets between Binance and BNB Chain and for crossings in the same millisecond as the block — are 0.4–5.4% of CEX-triggered arbitrages by arbitrage contracts in the core pools. Before Lorentz, blocks were stamped in whole seconds but also sealed on whole seconds, so no quantisation enters.

**Validation.** On simulated data — a random-walk reference with occasional jumps, a pool with a 5 bp band, three arbitrage contracts with latencies of 250, 500 and 900 ms competing for every opening, and 1.5-second blocks for two days followed by 0.75-second blocks for two days — the construction recovers the winners' latency distribution: quantile intercepts of 259–282 ms in both regimes for a true median of 267 ms, a Turnbull median of 250 ms, a median response time that falls by 373 ms against the mechanical 375 ms, and a latency-change component of +0.003. When the contracts' latencies are halved after the fork, the latency-change component becomes −0.134 and the paired contracts' tenth percentile of τ falls by 247 ms against a mechanical 75 ms (their intercepts by 172 ms). The simulation also reproduces the component pattern of Section 6.9 with no behaviour beyond a fixed latency: the movement component M falls by −0.37 log points at the fork (the √Δt law: −0.35; the effect implied by E[√τ]: −0.20) and the jump component by −0.07, because conditional on the opportunity surviving until the block the accumulated movement grows nearly in proportion to the response time rather than to its square root.

**Router flow.** The flow through public routers is concentrated in the two WBNB pools and in the Lorentz and Maxwell windows (Table D11 and Section 4.5). Including it in the response-time statistics lowers the tenth-percentile intercept of the WBNB/USDT 0.05% pool from 344 to 185 ms before Lorentz, from 246 to 60 ms after it and from 249 to 103 ms before Maxwell, and those of the 1 bp pool by 50–230 ms in the same regimes, while the intercepts of the ETH and BTCB pools change by at most 65 ms (and by less than 10 ms outside the Lorentz window); the aggregate strict-overshoot effect of Lorentz is −0.36 with it and −0.29 without it (Table 11). Tables 10, 11 and D1–D5 therefore use bot flow. The router flow that comes from persistent accounts (4–11% of strict arbitrages) has the same profile as the one-off flow — median overshoot 0.04–0.27 bp, 37–64% same-block — and is excluded with it; the cost is a small number of bots that call the SmartRouter directly from a stable account (in the Fermi window three accounts sent 92% of the SmartRouter's strict arbitrages, about 2% of all strict arbitrages). Operators can be merged only through shared signing accounts; a bot that used a fresh account for every transaction would appear as anonymous flow, and none of the large ones does.

## Appendix E. Reproducibility

All data are public: BNB Chain JSON-RPC (block headers, contract calls), Envio HyperSync and `eth_getLogs` (swap events) and Binance's public data archive (1-second candles and aggregate trades). The pipeline — timestamp reconstruction, event retrieval, alignment to both references, arbitrage identification, LP economics, hourly panels, regressions, matched pairs, placebos, the reference-wedge regressions, the three-fork stacking, the latency-floor fit, the reconstruction of opening and response times, the component panels, the retrieval of transaction senders and gas and the merging of contracts into operators, and the figures — is released as a public repository (see Code availability); each fork's full sample can be rebuilt from scratch on a laptop in a few hours, the aggregate-trade alignment adding 6–8 GB of downloads per fork.
