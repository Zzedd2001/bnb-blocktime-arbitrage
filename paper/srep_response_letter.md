## IV. The optional point: the response-time elasticity on subsamples with an unambiguous opening

The referee's concern was that, after volume and liquidity are held fixed, the only surviving evidence that "competition responds to volatility" was the fall of the response time τ with σ, and that the last-crossing convention that dates an opening could produce exactly such a fall when the reference price hovers at the band edge, which it does more often in volatile hours. We re-estimated the τ–σ relation on the two subsamples the referee named. Because sharp openings (crossing trade at least ½γ beyond the band edge) are about 1% of CEX-triggered bot arbitrages and on-chain-triggered arbitrages about 1–3% of them, the hourly panel of Table 3 is too thin for them, and the estimation is at the arbitrage level: log τ (and the indicator of landing in the first block) on the hour's log σ with hour-of-day and pool fixed effects within each regime, with and without the hour's log volume and log liquidity, day-clustered standard errors, three core pools pooled. The same regression on all CEX-triggered arbitrages reproduces Table 3 (log τ falls with σ at given volume and liquidity, −0.04 to −0.19), so the comparison is like for like. The results are Supplementary Table S5.

The elasticity does not survive. On sharp openings the coefficient on log σ at given volume and liquidity is +0.13 (0.15), +0.29 (0.15), −0.66 (0.19), −0.03 (0.12), +0.03 (0.16) and +0.23 (0.24) in the six regimes; on on-chain-triggered arbitrages, whose τ is a difference of two block timestamps and involves no reference price at all, it is −0.14 (0.12), +0.12 (0.05), +0.06 (0.07), +0.03 (0.05), +0.21 (0.11) and +0.00 (0.05), and the first-block indicator does not rise with σ on either subsample. Ten of these twelve estimates are within two standard errors of zero; the two that are not have opposite signs (sharp openings in the Maxwell pre-period, −0.66 (0.19); on-chain-triggered arbitrages in the Lorentz post-period, +0.12 (0.05)), so the subsamples give no consistent speed response to volatility. The two subsamples also have shorter response times and higher first-block shares than the full sample, which fits a mechanical reading of the full-sample elasticity: when the reference price crosses the band edge by a hair, the arbitrageur acts only once the deviation reaches its own threshold, and volatile hours cover that distance faster.

We have therefore rewritten the second paragraph of "Volatility and competition within the hour". It no longer claims that incumbents speed up in volatile hours. It reports the raw responses, states that holding volume and liquidity fixed removes most of the entry response and that the residual fall of the response times does not survive on the two clean subsamples, where ten of the twelve estimates are within two standard errors of zero and the other two have opposite signs (Supplementary Table S5), gives the mechanical reading above, and draws the conclusion that the evidence now supports: volatility brings volume and volume brings entrants, while the arbitrageurs' latency itself responds neither to the transient rise of the prize within the hour nor to the permanent, pre-announced change of the clock. This is a cleaner statement than the one it replaces, and it is the same statement the fork evidence makes. The Discussion did not rely on the within-hour speed response and is unchanged on this point; the first paragraph of the same subsection (the σ elasticities of the components M and J, which explain the aggregate elasticity of about 0.4) is unaffected, since it does not use τ. The script that produces Supplementary Table S5 (tau_sigma_clean.py) has been added to the replication package.
